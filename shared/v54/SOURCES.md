# V54 Sources

1. W. Stanley Jevons (1865), *The Coal Question*, Chapter VII, "Of the Economy of Fuel". Historical source for the efficiency/rebound argument later associated with the Jevons paradox.
   https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/jevons-the-coal-question

2. Daron Acemoglu & Pascual Restrepo (2019), *Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor*, Journal of Economic Perspectives / NBER Working Paper 25684.
   https://www.nber.org/papers/w25684

3. Daron Acemoglu, Fredric Kong & Pascual Restrepo (2024; revised 2025), *Tasks At Work: Comparative Advantage, Technology and Labor Demand*, NBER Working Paper 32872; Handbook of Labor Economics 2025.
   https://www.nber.org/papers/w32872

4. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li & Lindsey R. Raymond (2023; published 2025), *Generative AI at Work*, NBER Working Paper 31161 / Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(2), 889-942. Study of 5,179 customer-support agents; average productivity increase about 14%.
   https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

5. Fabrizio Dell'Acqua et al. (2026), *Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality*, Organization Science 37(2), 403-423. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2025.21838.
   https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838

## Editorial note
The video uses the Jevons paradox as an analogy for falling cognitive-production costs and expanding demand. It does not claim that every AI-enabled task or occupation must exhibit rebound, nor that AI necessarily increases total employment. The script explicitly separates employment headcount from workload per remaining worker and acknowledges displacement effects.
