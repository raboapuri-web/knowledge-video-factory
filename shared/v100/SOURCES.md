# V100 State Myths — scholarly grounding and political-neutrality safeguards

This video analyzes how modern states use shared narratives, rituals and collective memory. It does not endorse nationalism, anti-nationalism, any contemporary party, candidate, territorial claim, or particular state mythology. Treat "myth" analytically: a socially meaningful narrative can contain factual history, selective memory, symbolism, simplification, or contested interpretation; do not equate "myth" automatically with "lie."

- Benedict Anderson, *Imagined Communities*: nations are "imagined" because most members never meet one another, while print-capitalism and mass communication help people imagine shared simultaneity and community. Useful background summaries: Cambridge University Press discussions of Anderson and print-capitalism.
- Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, *The Invention of Tradition*: many practices presented as old can be comparatively recent, constructed or rapidly formalized; the concept is analytical, not a claim that all traditions are fraudulent.
- Robert N. Bellah, "Civil Religion in America" (Dædalus, 1967): argues that alongside churches exists a public civil-religious dimension organized around national symbols, rituals, historical figures and national purpose.
- Ernest Renan, "What Is a Nation?" (1882): emphasizes shared memories, present consent and the role of forgetting in national formation; his "daily plebiscite" formulation is a classic but historically situated argument, not a universal law.
- French Revolutionary festivals, including the 1794 Festival of the Supreme Being, are used as examples of revolutionary political ritual. Do not imply that all revolutionaries shared the same religious or symbolic program.
- Japan: the 1890 Imperial Rescript on Education and modern national schooling are discussed as part of Meiji state-building. Use MEXT historical materials and academic scholarship. Do not generalize this to timeless Japanese identity.
- "Civil religion", "imagined community" and "invented tradition" are distinct scholarly frameworks. Do not collapse them into one theory.
- Claims about shared narratives helping solidarity or redistribution should be presented as a mechanism or interpretation, not as a deterministic law. Institutions can function with multiple overlapping identities and forms of solidarity.
- The "mythless state" section is an explicit thought experiment, not a historical case or policy recommendation.
